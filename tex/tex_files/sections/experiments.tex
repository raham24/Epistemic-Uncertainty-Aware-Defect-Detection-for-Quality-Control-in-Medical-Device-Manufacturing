In this section, we present our numerical results and summarize the main findings through a series of plots.

We begin by examining how epistemic uncertainty affects the baseline benchmark introduced in \cite{shenoy2026uncertainty} and evaluate the extent to which our proposed approach mitigates its effects. Despite the relative simplicity of this task, it illustrates how models with otherwise strong predictive performance can remain susceptible to epistemic uncertainty, and how abstention can reduce the resulting decision risk. Furthermore, we introduce a more challenging variant of the original benchmark, in which the final detection task is more difficult by tweaking the data generative process, allowing us to evaluate the effectiveness of our mitigation approach under more realistic conditions.

Finally, we present a real-world medical device case study and introduce a benchmark that is publicly accessible through the \gls*{protookn}, demonstrating the practical applicability of our approach in a high-stakes setting.

In line with the literature, we report results over 5 different random seeds, controlling the models' initialization and the data splits. The results are reported in terms of \textbf{accuracy} or its converse, i.e., empirical \textbf{risk}. As we must account for the fact that the model may abstain on some samples, we also report the \textbf{coverage}, i.e., the fraction of samples on which the model makes a prediction. Importantly, note that in case of abstention, the accuracy and risk are only computed on the samples for which the model makes a prediction, i.e., the coverage. Thus, while our framework may abstain on some samples which may be correctly classified, \emph{increased accuracy and reduced risk} on the retained samples is a clear indication that \emph{the abstained set must contain a higher proportion of misclassified samples than the original dataset}, i.e. the abstention mechanism is preferentially rejecting predictions that are more likely to be incorrect.

For the synthetic experiments, we consider Head 1 of the multi-head cascade \gls*{mlp} architecture introduced in \cite{shenoy2026uncertainty}, which performs defect classification. The model consists of two hidden layers with 256 neurons each, ReLU activations, and a dropout rate of \(0.1\), and it is trained using Adam with a learning rate of \(10^{-3}\) and a batch size of 128 for up to 40 epochs, with early stopping using a patience of six epochs.

For the real-world experiments, the model consists of two hidden layers with 256 and 128 neurons, respectively, and ReLU activations, and it is trained using Adam with a learning rate of \(10^{-3}\), weight decay of \(10^{-4}\), and a batch size of 32 for up to 80 epochs, with early stopping using a patience of 10 epochs.

All models are implemented in PyTorch \cite{paszke2019pytorch}.

Development and preliminary experiments were conducted on Apple Silicon, while the main experimental sweeps were executed on a SLURM-based computing cluster using NVIDIA A100 and H100 GPUs, with one GPU allocated per run.

\subsection{Synthetic Benchmark}
\label{subsec:synthetic-benchmark}
We first consider the benchmark introduced in \cite{shenoy2026uncertainty}, which we refer to as the \emph{baseline}. To the best of our knowledge, the original benchmark data, which is also synthetic, has not been publicly released. We therefore reproduce the data-generation process following the description provided in the original work (cf.~\Cref{subsec:benchmark_gen,alg:generator}). To assess the fidelity of our reconstruction, we verify that a comparable \gls*{mlp} achieves performance consistent with that reported in the original work. Given the synthetic nature and relative simplicity of this benchmark, we primarily use it as a proof of concept to illustrate the effects of epistemic uncertainty and the benefits of abstention under controlled conditions. We subsequently introduce a more challenging synthetic scenario to evaluate whether these benefits persist as the underlying detection task becomes more complex.
\input{tex_files/synthetic_cascade_vs_abstention_fig}
The problem at hand considers a three-class classification task (\emph{no defect}, \emph{open circuit}, and \emph{solder bridging}) based on six process features: relative humidity, temperature, pressure, paste viscosity, stencil thickness, time above liquidus, and peak reflow temperature.
The per-feature class distributions are shown in \Cref{fig:baseline}. As can be observed, several features provide a relatively clear separation between classes, making the resulting classification task comparatively simple. Nevertheless, \Cref{fig:cascade-vs-abstention-synthetic} shows that abstaining on less than \(10\%\) of the samples yields an accuracy improvement of approximately \(4\) percentage points. This indicates that the abstention mechanism preferentially identifies predictions that are more likely to be misclassified, thereby improving the accuracy of the retained decisions.

Next, we artificially increase the difficulty of the classification task by increasing the overlap between the class-conditional feature distributions, thereby increasing the Bayes error, as illustrated in \Cref{fig:overlap_harder}. As expected, the overall classification accuracy decreases under this more challenging setting. Nevertheless, \Cref{fig:cascade-vs-abstention-synthetic} shows that abstention continues to improve the accuracy of the retained predictions, demonstrating the effectiveness of the approach even as the underlying classification problem becomes more difficult.

Finally, we present an ablation study across different target coverage levels in \Cref{fig:risk-baseline} for the baseline setting and \Cref{fig:risk-harder} for the higher-overlap setting. An additional ablation study across different payoff values, which further supports the results reported in \Cref{fig:selective-risk}, is provided in the appendix (cf.~\Cref{subsec:additional_numerical_results}).
