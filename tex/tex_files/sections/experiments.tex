In this section, we present our numerical results and summarize the main findings through a series of plots.

We begin by examining how epistemic uncertainty affects the baseline benchmark introduced in \cite{shenoy2026uncertainty} and evaluate the extent to which our proposed approach mitigates its effects. Despite the relative simplicity of this task, it illustrates how models with otherwise strong predictive performance can remain susceptible to epistemic uncertainty, and how abstention can reduce the resulting decision risk. Furthermore, we introduce a more challenging variant of the original benchmark, in which the final detection task is more difficult by tweaking the data generative process, allowing us to evaluate the effectiveness of our mitigation approach under more realistic conditions.

Finally, we present a real-world medical device case study and introduce a benchmark that is publicly accessible through the \gls*{protookn}, demonstrating the practical applicability of our approach in a high-stakes setting.

Following standard experimental practice, we report results over five independent runs with different random seeds to account for variability due to random initialization.
% we report results over 5 different random seeds, controlling the models' initialization and the data splits. 
The results are reported in terms of \textbf{accuracy} or its converse, i.e., empirical \textbf{risk}. As we must account for the fact that the model may abstain on some samples, we also report the \textbf{coverage}, i.e., the fraction of samples on which the model makes a prediction. Importantly, note that in case of abstention, the accuracy and risk are only computed on the samples for which the model makes a prediction, i.e., the coverage.

Thus, while our framework may abstain on some samples which may be correctly classified, \emph{increased accuracy and reduced risk} on the retained samples is a clear indication that \emph{the abstained set must contain a higher proportion of misclassified samples than the original dataset}, i.e. the abstention mechanism is preferentially rejecting predictions that are more likely to be incorrect.

For the synthetic experiments, we consider Head 1 of the multi-head cascade \gls*{mlp} architecture introduced in \cite{shenoy2026uncertainty}, which performs defect classification. The model consists of two hidden layers with 256 neurons each, ReLU activations, and a dropout rate of \(0.1\), and it is trained using Adam with a learning rate of \(10^{-3}\) and a batch size of 128 for up to 40 epochs, with early stopping using a patience of 6 epochs.

For the real-world experiments, the model consists of two hidden layers with 256 and 128 neurons, respectively, and ReLU activations, and it is trained using Adam \cite{kingma2014method} with a learning rate of \(10^{-3}\), weight decay of \(10^{-4}\), and a batch size of 32 for up to 80 epochs, with early stopping using a patience of 10 epochs.

All models are implemented in PyTorch \cite{paszke2019pytorch}. Development and preliminary experiments were conducted on Apple Silicon, while the main experimental sweeps were executed on a SLURM-based computing cluster using NVIDIA A100 and H100 GPUs, with one GPU allocated per run.

\subsection{Synthetic Benchmark}
\label{subsec:synthetic-benchmark}
We first consider the benchmark introduced in \cite{shenoy2026uncertainty}, which we refer to as the \emph{baseline}. To the best of our knowledge, the original benchmark data, which is also synthetic, has not been publicly released. We therefore reproduce the data-generation process following the description provided in the original work (cf.~\Cref{subsec:benchmark_gen,alg:generator}). To assess the fidelity of our reconstruction, we verify that a comparable \gls*{mlp} achieves performance consistent with that reported in the original work. Given the synthetic nature and relative simplicity of this benchmark, we primarily use it as a proof of concept to illustrate the effects of epistemic uncertainty and the benefits of abstention under controlled conditions. We subsequently introduce a more challenging synthetic scenario to evaluate whether these benefits persist as the underlying detection task becomes more complex.
\input{\projecttex/tex_files/synthetic_cascade_vs_abstention_fig}
The problem at hand considers a three-class classification task (\emph{no defect}, \emph{open circuit}, and \emph{solder bridging}) based on six process features: ambient relative humidity, ambient temperature, paste viscosity, stencil thickness, time above liquidus, and peak reflow temperature.
The per-feature class distributions are shown in \Cref{fig:baseline}. As can be observed, several features provide a relatively clear separation between classes, making the resulting classification task comparatively simple. Nevertheless, \Cref{fig:cascade-vs-abstention-synthetic} shows that when abstaining on less than \(10\%\) of the samples, the accuracy increases from \(95.10\%\) to \(98.17\%\), an improvement of \(3.07\) percentage points, corresponding to a \(62.65\%\) relative reduction in classification error. This indicates that the abstention mechanism preferentially identifies predictions that are more likely to be misclassified, thereby improving the accuracy of the retained decisions.

Next, we artificially increase the difficulty of the classification task by increasing the overlap between the class-conditional feature distributions, thereby increasing the Bayes error, as illustrated in \Cref{fig:overlap_harder}. As expected, the overall classification accuracy decreases under this more challenging setting. Nevertheless, \Cref{fig:cascade-vs-abstention-synthetic} shows that abstention continues to improve the accuracy of the retained predictions, demonstrating the effectiveness of the approach even as the underlying classification problem becomes more difficult. In this case, abstaining yields an accuracy increase from \(87.59\%\) to \(89.94\%\), an improvement of \(2.35\) percentage points, corresponding to an \(18.94\%\) relative reduction in classification error.

Finally, we present an ablation study across different target coverage levels in \Cref{fig:risk-baseline} for the baseline setting and \Cref{fig:risk-harder} for the higher-overlap setting. An additional ablation study across different payoff values, which further supports the results reported in \Cref{fig:selective-risk}, is provided in the appendix (cf. \Cref{subsec:additional_numerical_results,fig:ablation-k-synthetic}).

\subsection{Experiment on real medical devices data}
\label{subsec:real-medical-devices-data}

In this section, we present the results of our experiments on real-world medical device data. The dataset consists of report narratives, extracted from \cite{fda_maude,fda_510k} through the contributed \gls*{kg} within the \gls*{protookn} project (cf. \cite{protookn}).
\input{\projecttex/tex_files/maude_ablation_fig.tex}
The final dataset comprises 266,170 reports (185,477 malfunction, 44,995 injury, 33,897 serious injury, 1801 death), partitioned into training (64\%), validation (16\%), and test (20\%) splits and stratified by outcome class. Severity labels are derived from the MAUDE event type field as follows: reports typed Death and Malfunction map directly to the Death and Malfunction classes, while Injury reports are split into basic vs. serious injury. An injury is classified as serious if its narrative matches a serious-injury cue and basic injury otherwise\footnote{Keywords that identify serious injury are the ones that are described as life threatening situation, permanent impairment and surgical interventions.}, reports typed Other or No answer, or carrying no narrative are discarded. Sentence in the narratives which contain the labels or words closely match the labels are discarded. Duplicate reports were removed based on exact narrative text duplications.

These narratives consist of structured key--value pairs, including textual descriptions of adverse events associated with medical devices. The datasets are connected by the device identifiers, which are used to link the narratives to the corresponding device information.

The task we select is to classify these text-based features into four outcome classes: malfunction, basic injury, serious injury, and death, in increasing order of severity. The underlying objective is to identify patterns associated with adverse events in previously unseen data, with the broader goal of supporting their early detection and potentially preventing similar events from occurring in the future.
Text content is represented using TF--IDF features with up to 50,000 unigram and bigram features. Further details can be found in the code repository, which is made publicly available alongside the paper.

\input{\projecttex/tex_files/maude_barplot_fig.tex}

\Cref{fig:maude-k} and \Cref{fig:maude-coverage} show the effectiveness of abstention on the retained predictions, confirming the expected results. Additionally, for this task, it is possible to achieve almost perfect accuracy for \(\approx 70\%\) coverage.
This indicates that the abstention mechanism is preferentially rejecting predictions that are more likely to be incorrect, thereby improving the accuracy of the retained predictions, though nearly optimal accuracy may incur a high cost in terms of the number of predictions that are abstained from. The ablation study across different target coverage levels in \Cref{fig:maude-k} further supports this conclusion, demonstrating that the benefits of abstention persist across a range of coverage levels.

Finally, \Cref{fig:maude-noabstain-vs-abstain} shows the performance comparison between the standard cross-entropy model and its version equipped with abstention, showing consistent and favorable results across multiple independent runs.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{subsec:limitations-and-future-work}

Given the importance of uncertainty-aware decision-making in medical device manufacturing, this work represents an initial step toward systematically studying epistemic uncertainty in this domain. A key limitation is the current availability of real-world benchmarks that capture both manufacturing defects and the uncertainty associated with their detection. Consequently, broader evaluation across devices, defect types, and manufacturing settings remains necessary to establish the generalizability of these findings.

Two characteristics of the underlying data source should be kept in mind when interpreting these results. MAUDE is a passive, spontaneous-report surveillance system: report volumes reflect reporting behavior, device utilization, and regulatory reporting requirements rather than confirmed failure incidence, and narratives vary widely in completeness and may
contain duplicates~\cite{chung2020signal}. Accordingly, the benchmark task should be understood as report-level severity classification under realistic label noise, a regime in which epistemic uncertainty is expected to be substantial and abstention correspondingly valuable, and
not as an estimate of device failure rates. These characteristics make MAUDE-derived data a stringent, practically relevant testbed for uncertainty-aware classification~\cite{mao2021nlp,sohoni2026llm}.

We hope this work will encourage further research on epistemic uncertainty in medical device manufacturing, particularly through the development of larger, more diverse, and application-relevant benchmarks. Resources such as the \gls*{protookn}, the data repositories in \cite{fda_510k,fda_maude}, together with other tools for integrating and organizing heterogeneous medical device data, provide an opportunity to facilitate the construction and sharing of such datasets. Such efforts could support more comprehensive evaluations of uncertainty-aware methods and provide a stronger empirical foundation for their use in medical device quality assurance.
