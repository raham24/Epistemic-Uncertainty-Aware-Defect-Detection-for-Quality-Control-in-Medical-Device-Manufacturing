\subsection{Benchmark generation and difficulty controls}
\label{subsec:benchmark}

The benchmark is a \emph{family} of datasets, all produced by the generator of
\cref{alg:generator} from one base process specification, each under a different
\emph{difficulty configuration}. A configuration overrides at most three orthogonal
knobs; every other field of the spec is held fixed, so the regimes differ only along
the axis under study. \Cref{alg:benchmark} makes this concrete, and
\cref{tab:regimes} lists the regimes we use.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Benchmark suite generation}
\label{alg:benchmark}
\DontPrintSemicolon
\SetKwInOut{Input}{Input}\SetKwInOut{Output}{Output}
\SetKwComment{Comment}{// }{}
    \Input{base spec $\Theta_0=(\bm\mu,\bm\sigma_0,\mathbf R,\bm\delta,\mathbf W,\bm\pi_0,\varepsilon^\star_0,\rho)$ (the inputs of \cref{alg:generator}); difficulty configurations $\mathcal C=\{c_1,\dots,c_M\}$; boards per dataset $N$; seed $s$.}
    \Output{one labeled, split dataset $\mathcal D_c$ per configuration $c\in\mathcal C$.}
    \BlankLine
    \Comment{each configuration $c\in\mathcal C$ sets a subset of the three knobs: Bayes error $\varepsilon_c$, priors $\bm\pi_c$, spread scale $\alpha_c$; unset knobs default to the base spec ($\varepsilon^\star_0$, $\bm\pi_0$, $\alpha_c=1$)}
    $\bm\sigma_c \leftarrow \alpha_c\,\bm\sigma_0$\;
    \BlankLine
    \Comment{generate one dataset with \cref{alg:generator} ($=\textsc{GenerateData}$) under the overridden spec}
    $\mathcal D_c \leftarrow \textsc{GenerateData}(\bm\mu,\bm\sigma_c,\mathbf R,\bm\delta,\mathbf W,\bm\pi_c,\varepsilon_c,\rho,\,N,\,s)$\;
    \BlankLine
    \textbf{Return:} $\{\mathcal D_c\}_{c\in\mathcal C}$\;
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Tunable difficulty parameters.}
The three knobs act on separate stages of \cref{alg:generator} and are, by construction,
independent:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Target Bayes error $\varepsilon^\star$ (label difficulty).}
    The generator binary-searches the logit gain $\gamma$ so the realized Bayes error of
    the sampled labels equals $\varepsilon^\star$. A larger $\varepsilon^\star$ forces a
    smaller $\gamma$, which flattens the class posterior $\PYX(\cdot\mid\x)$ and increases
    class overlap --- the \emph{primary} difficulty axis, and the one that lowers the
    Bayes-optimal accuracy ceiling. The increased posterior overlap is exactly what
    \cref{fig:feature-separability} visualizes.
    \item \textbf{Class priors $\bm\pi$ (class balance).}
    Fit through the per-class offsets $\bm\beta$ (iterative proportional fitting), $\bm\pi$
    sets how skewed the label distribution is without touching the posterior sharpness.
    It stresses minority-class detection and class-weighting rather than the Bayes ceiling.
    \item \textbf{Process-spread scale $\alpha$ (physical spread).}
    $\alpha$ multiplies the parameter spreads $\bm\sigma$, widening the process
    distributions and thereby raising spec-violation rates and the graded-risk targets
    $r_j$. Crucially, because the gain $\gamma$ is \emph{recalibrated} to $\varepsilon^\star$
    \emph{after} scaling, the label difficulty is unchanged: the calibration absorbs the
    spread. $\alpha$ therefore probes the risk-regression head and process realism, not
    defect classification.
\end{itemize}

Feature separability is thus a consequence of $\varepsilon^\star$, not a separate control:
raising $\varepsilon^\star$ is what produces the overlapping feature distributions of the
harder regimes.
